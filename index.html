PLS 519 final project

Mapping Energy Futures

What do people in Kazakhstan’s coal and oil towns picture for 2050, and should AI be used to find out?

Verdict in one line

Only under conditions.
  • Process the data locally, inside Kazakhstan.
  • Use shared or rented in-country computing, not a new workstation.
  • The model describes; it does not interpret or infer.
  • Humans write the codebook and make every final decision.
  • A successful pilot first; otherwise human-only coding.
  • Consent, opt-out and ethics approval before fieldwork.

1 · In brief

In a nutshell

This project asks whether AI can help researchers understand how people in Kazakhstan’s coal, oil and other cities imagine their energy futures in 2050. The energy-transition context explains why these perspectives matter; the central question is whether an AI model can reliably analyse residents’ drawings without replacing human interpretation or introducing new biases.

The proposed research would invite approximately 700 residents across 12 cities to draw what they imagine their city and its energy system will look like in 2050. Participants would add a short caption in Kazakh or Russian. A vision-language model (VLM) would assist with identifying visible elements in the drawings, while local human coders would review its suggestions and make the final coding decisions.

The project does not assume that AI is necessary or that it will work. Its use would depend on a pilot testing whether model-assisted coding is sufficiently reliable, whether errors differ across city types, and whether appropriate computing infrastructure is available in Kazakhstan. If these conditions are not met, the research can proceed through human-only coding.

2 · Problem

The problem: Whose future counts in the energy transition?

Kazakhstan has pledged to achieve carbon neutrality by 2060. Yet its economy and electricity system remain heavily dependent on fossil fuels. This creates a difficult transition: moving away from coal, oil and gas is not simply a matter of replacing one energy source with another. It also affects employment, public revenue, local economies and the everyday lives of communities.

In 2025, coal accounted for 58.3% of Kazakhstan’s electricity generation, compared with 58.5% in 2024 and 70.4% in 2015 (Ember, n.d.). Gas increased from 17.3% of generation in 2015 to 27.1% in 2025. Wind, solar and bioenergy together accounted for 5.8% in 2025, based on the measure used here. The national target is to reach 15% renewable electricity by 2030, excluding large hydropower (IEA, 2022). Other sources put the coal share higher: about 65% for 2022 (UNECE, 2025) and 74.9% for the Unified Electric Power System in 2024 (Samruk-Energy, 2025), because the year, the grid covered and the definition differ.

The challenge is not only technological. Fossil fuels remain important to the national economy and to the livelihoods of particular regions. Twenty-seven of Kazakhstan’s 89 cities are classified as single-industry towns, home to about 1.4 million people and nearly 40% of industrial production (Satubaldina, 2021). Hydrocarbons accounted for more than 53% of exports and approximately 15% of GDP in 2023, according to the OECD (2024). These dependencies are unevenly distributed: some cities and regions rely much more heavily on mining and energy-related employment than others.

In 2025, mining and energy supply accounted for 4.8% of employment nationally (BNS, n.d.). The share was considerably higher in Mangystau (15.1%), Ulytau (12.0%), Aktobe (11.9%) and Karaganda (10.8%). These figures use the broader mining and quarrying category, which includes metal ores as well as fossil-fuel extraction.

Employment is only one part of the picture. Mining wages are higher than the median wage across all sectors in every region in the dataset. In some regions, the difference is substantial: the mining median is approximately 3.7 times the all-sector median in Atyrau and 3.8 times in West Kazakhstan (BNS, n.d.).

These differences matter because a transition away from fossil fuels may have very different implications for people depending on where they live and how they earn a living. A new energy project may create opportunities in one place while threatening established jobs or local revenue in another.

The question, then, is not only how Kazakhstan should transition, but whose expectations and concerns shape that transition. Do residents of coal, oil and industrial towns imagine a different future from people living in larger, more diversified cities? And are these visions visible in the discussions and decisions that shape energy policy?

3 · Data

What the data cannot tell us

National and regional statistics help establish where economic dependence on fossil fuels is concentrated. They cannot, however, tell us how residents understand these changes or what they want their cities to become.

The evidence used here comes from several sources: the Bureau of National Statistics (BNS) for employment, wages, population and heating; Ember for electricity generation, demand and emissions; the IEA, OECD and UNECE for the energy and economic context; and Samruk-Energy’s 2024 annual report for the industry view of the grid. These sources do not always agree. Coal supplied 58.3% of electricity in Ember’s 2025 data, about 65% for 2022 in UNECE (2025), and 74.9% for the Unified Electric Power System in 2024 in Samruk-Energy (2025). The differences come from the year, the grid covered and the definition used, so any single figure should be read together with its source and scope.

Existing surveys offer some insight into public awareness, knowledge and attitudes towards energy transition policies (for example, Junussova, 2026). But they generally use predefined questions and answer options. They do not necessarily allow participants to describe their own visions of the future, including possibilities that researchers may not have anticipated.

There are also important limitations in the available data:

  • Geographic detail: Many official statistics are published at the oblast level rather than the city level. Several study cities therefore share the same regional figures.
  • Employment categories: Mining and quarrying includes metal ores, not only fossil fuels. It is therefore an imperfect measure of dependence on coal, oil and gas.
  • Energy poverty: The available national statistics do not directly measure energy poverty at the city level. Existing research provides estimates, but these cannot be treated as a direct measure of each city's situation.
  • Public expression: Political sensitivities and the possibility of self-censorship may affect how people discuss energy, employment and the future (see Freedom House, 2025).

The project addresses a different question from existing surveys: rather than asking residents to respond to predefined policy options, it invites them to represent their own imagined futures through drawings and short descriptions.

4 · Research

Current state of research

Research on Kazakhstan's energy transition has examined public awareness, attitudes towards renewable energy and the potential social consequences of decarbonisation. Studies have also highlighted concerns about employment and the uneven distribution of transition risks (Junussova, 2026). Neafie et al. (2025) describe Kazakhstan as a centralised, extractives-dependent state whose renewable ambitions may clash with local sustainability goals. However, existing research has not established how residents of different types of cities imagine their own energy futures.

A second strand of literature focuses on energy justice and sociotechnical imaginaries. Energy justice draws attention to how the costs and benefits of energy systems are distributed, whose voices are included in decision-making, and how past harms are addressed (McCauley & Heffron, 2018). The concept of sociotechnical imaginaries helps explain how shared visions of social life are connected to expectations about technology (Jasanoff & Kim, 2009). This project applies these ideas to the futures imagined by residents themselves.

Participatory methods, including workshops, visioning exercises and draw-and-write activities (Angell et al., 2015), offer ways to elicit perspectives that conventional surveys may miss. Drawings can make complex ideas visible, while accompanying captions allow participants to explain what they intended to represent. Yet these approaches are often small-scale and require substantial human effort to analyse.

Meanwhile, research on AI-assisted text analysis has shown that language models can help researchers classify large volumes of material (Dunivin, 2025), but their outputs can be inconsistent and biased. Evidence on AI interpretation of images is less developed. Existing studies have examined student drawings, system maps and other visual materials (Lee & Zhai, 2025; Sheshadri et al., 2025; White & Barbrook-Johnson, 2026), but these are not equivalent to drawings in which adults imagine the future of their own cities.

The gap is therefore twofold: we know relatively little about how residents of different Kazakhstani cities imagine their energy futures, and we do not yet know whether vision-language models can reliably analyse this kind of material. The project treats the second question as a validity test, not an assumption that AI will provide a better answer.

5 · Solution

The AI-assisted solution

The proposed use of AI is methodological, not predictive. The model is not being asked to imagine Kazakhstan's energy future or decide what residents want. It is being tested as a tool for analysing drawings produced by residents themselves.

The project would collect approximately 700 drawings from residents of 12 cities, representing five city types: large metropolitan, coal, oil and gas, industrial, and transitional. Participants would be asked to draw their city and its energy system in 2050 and add a short caption in Kazakh or Russian.

The analysis would follow several steps:

  1. Develop a codebook. Local coders would establish categories for analysing the drawings, pilot the coding process and refine the categories before larger-scale analysis.
  2. Use AI for descriptive coding. A vision-language model would be asked to identify visible elements, such as energy sources, infrastructure, buildings and environmental features. It would receive both the drawing and its caption as context.
  3. Keep interpretation with human coders. The model would not infer emotions, political positions or who benefits from the transition. More interpretive categories, including who appears to benefit or be left out, would be assessed by human coders using the drawing and the participant's caption. The model may suggest descriptive codes, but human coders would review and determine the final coding.
  4. Test reliability. Model-assisted coding would be compared with human coding using Cohen's kappa, with model-human agreement assessed against human-human agreement. The project would also examine whether errors differ across city types.
  5. Apply a pilot threshold. The project would only proceed to larger-scale AI-assisted coding if the pilot meets a pre-established reliability threshold. If it does not, the research would use human-only coding.
  6. Review the results. If the project proceeds, coders would review model suggestions, including a random sample and cases where repeated model runs disagree. Human coders would retain responsibility for the final codes.

The proposed arrangement involves one researcher and one local coder in each study city. Drawings would be processed using computing infrastructure located in Kazakhstan. The project would publish aggregate findings by city type rather than individual responses.

The aim is not to automate interpretation. It is to test whether AI can support a consistent descriptive coding process while keeping contextual and interpretive decisions under human control.

There are a few studies that could be applicable to my project, each showing a different side of using AI to understand what people think and imagine. Gul et al. (2024) used photovoice with 26 students in India and analysed the photos and captions with AI, showing that participant-made images can be turned into useful themes, though only as a small proof of concept. Sheshadri et al. (2025) tested three multimodal AI models on hand-drawn community charts with local-language text and found frequent misreadings, mistranslations and invented details, which directly supports keeping local human coders in charge and piloting the AI before relying on it. Jigsaw’s Bowling Green project (2025) used AI to sort ideas from nearly 8,000 residents into topics within minutes, showing the scale and time savings possible when local people supply the context the AI lacks. Taken together, these studies support an approach that pilots the AI first, keeps humans making the final coding decisions, and falls back to human-only coding if the AI is not reliable enough.

6 · Costs

What it could cost

The project has three main cost dimensions: environmental, economic and social.

Environmental cost

The estimated operational emissions from running the model are relatively small. The calculation assumes 700 drawings, three model runs per drawing, and approximately 30 seconds of processing per run. This amounts to around 17.5 GPU-hours.

For a workstation drawing between 0.35 and 0.67 kW, the estimated electricity use is approximately 6.1–11.7 kWh. Using the 2025 grid-emissions intensity of 812.8 g CO₂/kWh (emissions divided by generation, 2025; Ember, n.d.), this corresponds to approximately 5.0–9.5 kg of operational CO₂ emissions. For scale, 11.7 kWh is less than one ten-billionth of Kazakhstan’s 125.5 TWh of electricity demand in 2025.

This is a limited estimate, not a full lifecycle assessment (see Luccioni et al., 2024). It excludes water use, embodied emissions from hardware and emissions associated with fieldwork travel. The latter may be considerably larger, but is not quantified here.

Economic cost

The estimated costs differ depending on how the analysis is conducted:

  • Human-only coding: Approximately 1.40 million KZT, including estimated fieldwork travel and human coding.
  • Closed API: The model-processing cost itself could be very low, but using an external API would mean that drawings and captions leave Kazakhstan. This conflicts with the project's proposed data-localisation requirement.
  • Local open-weight model: Purchasing a dedicated workstation is estimated to cost 2.27–2.95 million KZT. Including fieldwork and coding, the total would be approximately 3.67–4.35 million KZT.

All figures in this section are the author’s calculations, using the sources cited below and the assumptions set out in the project spreadsheet, which is available on this site. These estimates are a budget floor. They exclude several costs, including the pilot, prompt development, coder training, facilitators, venues, printing, participant incentives, consent translation and researcher time.

Download the cost calculations (Excel): inputs, city data and formulas on separate sheets

The comparison suggests that the main financial challenge is not the cost of running the model, but the infrastructure required to keep data processing within Kazakhstan. A shared or rented in-country computing resource would therefore be preferable to purchasing a workstation for this project alone.

Who pays and who profits: in this design the project’s funder (Nazarbayev University, President’s WA) pays for travel, coders and any equipment. The equipment spending would go to hardware and compute vendors, and a closed API would send revenue to a foreign provider. The local coders would be paid for their review work, while participant incentives are not covered in this budget.

Labour cost

The project does not assume that AI will reduce the overall need for human labour. Local coders would still review model suggestions and determine the final codes.

The current labour comparison is illustrative rather than measured. It assumes that manual coding takes four minutes per drawing and that AI-assisted review could reduce this to two minutes. Neither estimate has yet been tested. The pilot would measure the time required for both approaches, including time spent correcting model suggestions and resolving disagreements.

If AI does not reduce the overall workload, there would be no labour-saving justification for its use. The project may still find that AI improves consistency, but this would need to be demonstrated rather than assumed.

The analysis also needs to account for the often-invisible human labour involved in developing and maintaining AI systems (Williams & Miceli, 2023). The use of AI does not eliminate human work; it can redistribute it.

Governance and ethical considerations

The drawings and captions may contain sensitive information about local infrastructure, employment, environmental conditions or political concerns. Even without names, a recognisable street, facility or other local detail could make a participant identifiable. The possibility of self-censorship also needs to be considered.

The proposed safeguards include:

  • Written consent in Kazakh or Russian, explaining AI processing, server location and data retention.
  • The option to participate without having a drawing processed by AI.
  • No participation by minors.
  • Local transcription and deletion of audio recordings, if audio is collected.
  • Review by Nazarbayev University's Institutional Research Ethics Committee before fieldwork.
  • No use of real participant data in public chatbots or external AI services.
  • Publication of aggregate findings only, subject to a minimum group-size threshold.

The project would also need to consider how data are governed and who may access them. Local processing is intended to reduce the risks associated with transferring sensitive material outside Kazakhstan, but it does not remove the need for clear consent, access controls and responsible data management.

Governance and data protection

The project raises concerns about surveillance, misuse and loss of control over sensitive community data. Buribayeva et al. (2026) highlight how centralised data infrastructure, institutional practices and weak safeguards can create AI governance risks in Kazakhstan. The project therefore proposes local data processing and clear limits on data access.

Mhlambi (2020) advocates an approach to AI governance based on relational personhood, suggesting that consent should consider not only individual participants but also the communities involved. Roberts et al. (2024) discuss global AI governance; I infer that, in Kazakhstan, the project must also rely on safeguards that can be established and enforced domestically.

Two points are specific to this project. First, drawings about energy and local politics could be misused if third parties or authorities gained access to them. Kazakhstan scores 37 out of 100 on internet freedom (Freedom House, 2025), so the project would restrict who can see raw drawings, keep them on a machine without a public-network connection and publish only aggregates. Second, control: an open-weight model run by the project leaves decisions about data and model behaviour with local researchers, whereas a closed API leaves them with a foreign vendor. That is a further reason to prefer local processing.

Kazakhstan’s Law No. 230-VIII on Artificial Intelligence (signed 17 November 2025) provides the legal frame. Article 17(3)(6) prohibits determining a person’s emotions without consent, and Article 17(4) requires “local” systems, which run on the owner’s own infrastructure with no public-network connection, for restricted-access data. I found no article that requires data to stay in Kazakhstan, so the local-processing condition below is a design choice made for this project, not a legal requirement. The ban on emotion inference used here is also stricter than the law, which allows it with consent.

7 · Verdict

Verdict: only under conditions

Should AI be used to analyse how people in Kazakhstan imagine their energy futures?

Only under specific conditions. The available evidence does not yet justify assuming that a vision-language model can interpret residents' drawings reliably. But the research question is worth investigating, and AI may offer a useful way to test consistency in descriptive coding.

The proposed use of AI is conditional on six requirements:

  1. Local processing: Drawings, captions and any audio must remain in Kazakhstan. External APIs are not suitable under the proposed data-localisation requirement.
  2. Shared computing infrastructure: The project should use shared or rented in-country computing resources rather than purchase a dedicated workstation solely for this study.
  3. Descriptive rather than interpretive use: The model may identify visible elements but should not infer emotions, political positions or who benefits from the transition.
  4. Human control: Human coders must develop the codebook, assess interpretive categories and make all final coding decisions.
  5. A successful pilot: Model-human agreement must be sufficiently close to human-human agreement, and the project must not find systematic differences in errors across city types. The threshold should be established before the pilot results are assessed.
  6. Ethical safeguards: Participants must understand how their material will be processed, be able to opt out of AI processing, and have their privacy protected. Ethical approval must be obtained before fieldwork.

There is also a clear fallback rule:

If shared in-country computing is unavailable, the project should proceed with human-only coding. Purchasing a dedicated workstation would raise the estimated total cost to approximately 3.67–4.35 million KZT, compared with around 1.40 million KZT for the human-only approach. Since the proposed AI workflow has not demonstrated sufficient labour savings to recover that investment, buying new hardware cannot currently be justified on efficiency grounds alone.

Similarly, if the pilot reveals systematic errors, inconsistent results across repeated runs or substantial differences in accuracy between city types, the project should use human-only coding regardless of whether computing infrastructure is available.

The argument for AI is therefore limited. It is not that AI is cheaper, more objective or inherently better than human analysis. Its potential value lies in testing whether it can support a consistent descriptive coding process at a larger scale. If it cannot meet that standard, the research question remains worth pursuing without it.

What would change the verdict?

  • Towards using AI: Pilot results close to the human-human agreement level, no systematic differences in error rates across city types, and access to shared in-country computing.
  • Towards rejecting AI: Uneven performance across city types, frequent disagreement between repeated model runs, inadequate consent arrangements or inability to meet ethical and data-governance requirements.

Ultimately, the project asks two questions: what futures do residents imagine for their cities, and can AI help researchers understand those visions responsibly? The first question matters regardless of the answer to the second.
